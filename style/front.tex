\chapter*{Abstract}
\thispagestyle{front}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Large language models have become general-purpose tools for writing, learning, search and conversational assistance, and they are used by children and adolescents in contexts involving education, emotional support and companionship. That use has developed alongside growing provider interest in age verification, age prediction and age-differentiated safety mechanisms. Prior work has documented explicit safety failures in these systems, but the relationship between safety behaviour, linguistic accessibility and user age remains underexplored, and no existing benchmark varies the user's age while holding the request fixed. This study introduces one, grounded in the duties the Online Safety Act 2023 places on user-to-user services, and applies it to six commercial models from five providers across two experiments. The Adaptation experiment holds 200 scenarios constant and varies only the age the user discloses, across eight stated ages and four implicit cue conditions, yielding 46,800 requests of which 46,640 returned. The Persistence experiment tests whether whatever conditioning is observed survives three adversarial pressure methods over multi-turn dialogue.

Within the Adaptation experiment, safety and linguistic accessibility are analysed as separate dimensions of one behaviour, and they follow different age profiles. Refusal on age-restricted requests is 45.5 percentage points higher for a stated minor age than a stated adult age, and the single step from seventeen to eighteen accounts for roughly half of the whole change across the ladder from seven to twenty-one, on five of the six models. Reading level moves in the same direction but gradually: text for a stated minor is 1.77 grade levels lower, and the same seventeen to eighteen step accounts for only three to ten per cent of each model's full range. Decomposing that reduction shows it is carried almost entirely by syllables per word rather than by sentence length. An explicit age statement moves behaviour substantially further than an implicit cue of the same age. Crossing the stated decision with the delivered content also separates models that agree and then supply nothing from models that are genuinely permissive, which a refusal rate alone reports identically. These results establish that responses are conditioned on disclosed age, and that they are conditioned unevenly across dimensions; they do not establish that a model inferred the user's age or applied any particular rule, and detecting age is therefore not by itself evidence that a system will adapt appropriately.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} large language models; child safety; age
conditioning; readability; multi-turn dialogue; AI regulation.
